# NASA GRC — Combustion research on the ISS (NP-2015-10-034-JSC, 2015)

Source: NASA Glenn Research Center mini-book *Combustion in Reduced Gravity*, document NP-2015-10-034-JSC (2015). This is a 2015 snapshot. Hardware described as planned (ACME inserts, SoFIE, some Cool Flame work) must be checked against NASA PSI records for what actually flew. Use PSI IDs when stating that an investigation has data.

## Page 9 — Prevention, detection and suppression

Fire safety is an operational element of human spaceflight. Every aspect of fire prevention, detection and suppression is fundamentally different in reduced gravity than on Earth. Gravity-related combustion issues matter for crewed habitats, not only for terrestrial engines.

## Page 19 — MSG air and exploration atmospheres

BASS used the SPICE flow duct in the ISS Microgravity Science Glovebox. The glovebox is normally limited to ISS cabin air, nominally 21 percent oxygen by volume and one atmosphere; nitrogen was later used down to about 17 percent oxygen. SoFIE (then planned) targeted a chamber of 10–40 percent oxygen by volume at 0.2–1.5 atm.

## Page 21 — Spacecraft ventilation 0–30 cm/s

Space Exploration Atmospheres relevant to NASA can be investigated with airflows in the spacecraft ventilation range of 0 to 30 cm/s. BASS tests the hypothesis that materials in microgravity with adequate ventilation burn as well as, if not better than, the same material in normal gravity at otherwise identical conditions.

## Page 22 — Nitrogen jets can strengthen a flame

For a fire in microgravity, the best point to apply suppressant is not obvious. Depending on flame geometry and extinguisher characteristics, the suppressant stream can be ineffective or can make the flame worse by entraining oxygen. Early BASS results showed nitrogen applied to the flame can actually strengthen it. SoFIE was in science definition in 2015 (CIR insert and large flow duct); confirm flight status in PSI.

## Page 26 — Detection (SAME, DAFT) and water-mist extinguishers

Spacecraft fire detection is harder because buoyancy changes smoke properties. DAFT measured background aerosols on the ISS. SAME measured the particle-size distribution of smoke from spacecraft materials. Those instruments guide future detector design. After the Mist experiment (STS-107, 2003), non-toxic water-mist portable extinguishers were certified; the first units were sent to the ISS in December 2015 to replace CO₂ extinguishers.

## Page 31 — Flammability map and three operational facts

Figure 8 (schematic): for flame spread over solids, the y-axis is ambient oxygen mole fraction and the x-axis is gas velocity across the surface. Inside the boundary, steady flame spread occurs; outside it does not. The minimum of the curve is on the order of 10 cm/s — typical spacecraft ventilation — and coincides with a maximum flame-spread rate. At low forced flow in microgravity, a thin fuel ignited in the middle can spread upstream into the oncoming air, unlike 1g. BASS on the ISS indicated NASA-STD-6001 Test 1 is not conservative: materials can burn at lower oxygen in microgravity than on Earth. Local application of suppressant is not adequate: the jet entrains air and sustains the flame even when ambient airflow is turned off.

## Page 35 — Three misconceptions and smoke detectors

NASA lists three misconceptions that research overturned: (1) a steady fire cannot exist in quiescent microgravity; (2) a fire will self-extinguish if ventilation is shut off; (3) because reduced gravity is inherently safer, terrestrial fire standards are conservative. Microgravity flames have a different particulate size, so terrestrial detectors would be looking for the wrong signature. A GRC device that monitors particulates and chemical species together outperformed current aircraft smoke detectors in FAA joint tests (false-alarm rejection and real-event detection).

## Page 36 — FLEX droplet LOI and cool flames

FLEX in the CIR/MDCA studies spherically symmetric droplet combustion to identify limiting oxygen indices and inert-gas suppressant efficacy. Experiments identified radiative and diffusive extinction limits for heptane and methanol and an LOI significantly lower than in normal gravity and lower than previously predicted. After radiative extinction of the visible heptane flame, stable quasi-steady cool flames were observed (Nayagam et al., 2012).
